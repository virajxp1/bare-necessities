Planning a five-day wedding weekend means an overwhelming amount of logistics:
vendor payments split across multiple families, guest RSVPs for event after
event, seating charts, photo groups, and items moving between venues. Instead
of wrestling with a pile of spreadsheets, we built a custom planning app and
let AI tools do the heavy lifting. I provided the direction, decisions, and
specific requirements; the AI built the software and helped run the data.

The surprise was how much it changed *decision-making* rather than just
record-keeping. The best part of the app wasn't a dashboard. It was that any
painful, tedious change turned into a single sentence.

## 1. Start with one painful problem

We didn't try to build everything at once. Six months before the wedding, we
started with the single highest-friction problem: **the budget**.

When multiple families are splitting costs across several events, spreadsheets
get messy fast. Every expense has multiple details to track:

- Which specific event it belongs to (reception, sangeet, etc.)
- What category it fits (venue, decor, catering)
- Who actually paid for it
- How the final cost is split between families

The crucial detail was separating "who paid" from "who owes." Once those lived
in different fields instead of being blurred together, everyone had complete
clarity on the numbers from day one — even as thousands of dollars moved
around.

**Key takeaway:** AI works best when you hand it one specific, well-defined
problem first, not "build me an app."

## 2. One guest, one record

After the budget came everything else, and the natural instinct is a
spreadsheet per topic: one for the guest list, one for seating, one for RSVPs,
one for travel, one for photo groups. That's how you end up with twenty files
linking to each other, all slightly out of sync.

Instead, we moved everything into one database with a simple rule: the app is
the only official source of truth. The design that made everything else work
was this — **everything we knew about a person lived in one record.** Their
family, which events their RSVP covered, where they're seated, which photo
group they're in, when they arrive and leave, whether they need an airport
pickup, which welcome bag is theirs. One guest, one record, one place to look.

That structure is what made the app feel effortless later. Regular backups
meant nothing could be lost, and built-in checks verified that guest counts
and seating details actually matched across events before anything was
finalized.

## 3. Ask in plain language

This is where it paid off. When all of a guest's information lives in one
well-structured place, you can just *talk* to the app through its built-in AI
assistant:

- **Ask instead of hunt.** "When is my aunt flying in?" gets answered
  instantly — versus trying to remember which of twenty spreadsheets holds the
  itinerary, and whether it's current.
- **Let it flag gaps.** The assistant could tell us things we hadn't thought
  to ask: "your aunt is missing an itinerary," or "these guests don't have a
  table yet." Catching those in September beat discovering them the night
  before.
- **Make broad strokes.** "We're going to break up this table and spread
  everyone across three others" — the kind of reshuffle that would normally be
  an hour of painstaking spreadsheet surgery, cross-referencing five tabs,
  happened in one sentence plus a quick review.
- **Absorb last-minute chaos.** When an RSVP flipped a week out, one change
  cascaded: the seat opened up, the photo group updated, the headcount for
  vendors shifted. Instead of chasing five files, it was one conversation.

That last one matters most. Weddings change constantly and at the worst
possible times, and the cost of *adjusting* is what makes them stressful.
When adjusting costs one sentence, you actually keep up.

## 4. Keep the money visible

A wedding moves serious money around, and forecasting spend is genuinely hard:
deposits here, balances due there, commitments not yet paid, costs shared
between families in different ways.

Keeping the budget in the app meant we could always see how things were
moving: what was committed, what had been paid, what was coming up, and who
was paying for it. When a cost changed, we saw the ripple across the weekend
instead of discovering it at checkout. Deciding what to pay next, what we owed,
and whose card it went on became a quick lookup rather than a group
negotiation. That alone removed one of the biggest headaches of the entire
planning process.

## 5. Let AI draft, humans decide

As the date got closer, we added the assistant for making changes too:
"move these three families to table 7 and update the photo groups."

We designed it so **the AI could never edit anything directly.** Every request
produced a proposed plan — a clear before-and-after summary of every change it
would make. I reviewed the summary, clicked **Apply** if it looked right, or
discarded it if something was off.

That human-in-the-loop setup eliminated hundreds of small data-entry chores
while making sure the AI couldn't make an accidental mistake on data that
mattered.

## 6. What we learned

If you're planning a wedding, a major move, or any large event:

1. **Replace your most annoying spreadsheet first.** The file you dread
   updating is the best candidate for automation.
2. **Structure data around people, not topics.** One complete record per
   guest — not twenty linked spreadsheets — is what makes every other step
   possible.
3. **Ask in plain language, and let the AI flag what you forgot to ask.**
   Answering questions is useful; surfacing gaps proactively is better.
4. **Back up before you trust.** Never let AI manage important data without an
   easy restore option.
5. **Review before applying.** Let AI draft every change, but keep final
   approval in human hands.
6. **AI manages data; humans make the decisions.** The AI removed hundreds of
   small administrative headaches, but every family decision, seating choice,
   and personal detail stayed entirely ours.

What I remember most isn't the software. It's that when plans changed at the
last minute — and they did, more than once — fixing them took a sentence
instead of an evening.
